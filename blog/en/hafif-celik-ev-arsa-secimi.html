What Should You Check When Buying Land for a Light Steel House?

The most critical decision in building a house is not, as most people assume, choosing a model — it is buying the right plot. The wrong plot stops even the best project in the world before it starts. In this article we set out, in order, which documents to ask for and which questions to ask before you buy a plot.

🏟
⚠ Important Note

This article is general information and is not binding. Planning legislation and practice vary by province, district and plan status, and are updated over time. For your own plot, always obtain written information from the relevant municipality or provincial administration; on title deed matters, consult a specialist. Nothing in this article constitutes an undertaking on the part of MefSteel; the scope, technical specification, duration and price of each project are determined solely by the signed contract and its annexes.

1. What Does the Title Deed Say? (Classification)

The first thing to look at is the "classification" section of the title deed. That single word determines the whole process:

What the deed saysWhat it means
Building plotUsually within a development plan and open to building. The most straightforward option.
FarmlandAgricultural classification. Building may be possible, but it depends on additional conditions and permissions.
Vineyard / orchard / olive groveAlso agricultural in nature; may have its own specific legislation.

"Farmland" does not automatically mean "no house can be built" — but the process is more complex than on a zoned plot. Factors such as whether the plot lies inside or outside a village settlement area , its size and its road frontage are decisive. Because these conditions vary by area there is no single right answer: you have to ask the relevant authority.

2. Always Ask for the Zoning Status Document

The zoning status document is the official record showing what may be built on that plot. Rely on this document, not on the seller's word. It is obtained from the municipality's planning department and states the following:

InformationWhat it tells you
Permitted useResidential, commercial or agricultural — can you build a house?
TAKS (ground coverage ratio)What percentage of the plot the house footprint may cover
KAKS / floor area ratioWhat the total construction area of all floors may be
Number of storeysHow many storeys you may build
Setback distancesHow far back you must stay from the neighbouring boundaries and the road
Road situationWhether the plot has road frontage and how wide the road is
⚠ The Most Expensive Mistake

Buying land without checking the zoning status, on the assumption that "we will build somehow". Once setbacks and TAKS are combined, a 500 m² plot can leave a far smaller footprint than you imagined. Before you buy the plot, see the zoning status and have it worked out whether the house you have in mind actually fits there.

3. Road Frontage and Access

If a plot has no frontage onto an official road, the permit process becomes seriously difficult — and in some cases impossible. De facto access such as "we cross the neighbour's field" does not count as legal frontage.

There is also a practical matter specific to light steel: the panels are delivered to site by truck and a crane or forklift is usually used during assembly. A very narrow, very steep or impassable road affects the delivery and assembly plan. When you go to view the plot, ask this: can a truck get in here easily?

4. Infrastructure: Electricity, Water, Road, Sewage

You need infrastructure to be able to live in the house once it is finished, and this item is often forgotten in the budget. Check before you buy:

InfrastructureThe question to ask
ElectricityHow far away is the nearest line, is a connection possible?
WaterIs there mains water, or will a well or tank be needed?
SewageIs there a mains connection, or will a septic system be needed?
RoadIs the road open, is it accessible in winter?
Internet / telephoneWhat is the coverage like (especially on remote plots)

On a remote plot, having an electricity line brought in can sometimes cost as much as part of the house. Finding out about this item up front is better than being surprised later.

5. Ground and Slope

A light steel structure is far lighter than reinforced concrete, which is generally an advantage for the foundation. Even so, the foundation is designed to suit the ground, and the soil survey process is part of the project. When looking at a plot, watch out for:

  • Slope: On very sloping land, retaining and cut-and-fill costs rise.
  • Water: A plot that holds water in winter, or lies near a stream bed, needs extra measures.
  • Made ground: Land that has been filled in later may require additional ground works.
  • Neighbouring buildings: The condition of nearby buildings tells you something about the area.

If you want to read about ground conditions in more detail: Light Steel House Foundation and Soil Survey Guide.

6. Any Surprises in the Land Registry Record?

A record check at the land registry office looks at: mortgages, liens, annotations, easements, expropriation notes and shared ownership . If one of these exists it does not mean the land cannot be bought — but you should buy knowing what it is.

Shared title deeds in particular need care: the property belongs to several people and who uses which part is not physically separated on the deed. That can create additional conditions both in the permit process and in a mortgage application. If you are considering such a property, consult a specialist before buying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I buy the land first or choose the design first?

Usually the land first. The size, number of storeys and position of the house are set by the plot's zoning conditions. With a plot in hand a design can be adapted; fitting a ready-made design onto any plot may not be possible.

Can a two-storey light steel house be built on a small plot?

Yes, if your zoning status allows it in terms of storeys and floor area ratio. We covered the single-storey versus two-storey question separately here: One Storey or Two?

Who should come with me when I view land?

If possible, someone from the company that will build. Assessing the zoning status document together with whether the house really fits there is better than saying later "it turns out this house does not work here". For title and legal matters, it is also wise to consult a specialist.
